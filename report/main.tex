\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background and Context}

At Thapar, most buying and selling between students happens
in hostel WhatsApp groups, batch chats and the odd poster on
a noticeboard. These work well enough between friends, but
they were never meant for hundreds of students who don't
know each other to trade safely.

The same items change hands every semester. Seniors sell
their textbooks, calculators, drafters, cycles and lab coats,
and the incoming batch needs exactly those things. Because
this happens again and again inside the same small
community, even a small improvement in trust and visibility
adds up to a lot of trades over a year.

CampusXchange is our answer to this. It is a mobile
marketplace only for Thapar students. Everyone who uses it is
verified as a student, every listing is checked by an
administrator before it goes public, and every deal follows
a fixed path. The buyer and seller agree on a price through
structured offers, the payment is held in escrow, the item is
handed over at a campus meeting point after scanning a QR
code, and both sides photograph the item's condition. For
borrowed items there is also a verified return. This report
describes the system as we have built and deployed it by the
end of the semester.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

We identified four problems with the way students trade
today.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No single place to look.} A listing posted in
    one hostel group or one batch chat rarely reaches anyone
    outside that circle, so buyers and sellers who would
    happily trade never find each other.
  \item \textbf{No way to know who you are dealing with.}
    Anyone can be added to these groups, including people
    with no link to the college. There is no reliable way to
    check that the other person is a current student before
    money or goods change hands.
  \item \textbf{Lending is not tracked.} Every exchange is
    treated as a one-off sale. Nobody records who borrowed
    what, when it is due back or whether the deposit was
    returned, so students rarely lend things out, even
    though lending suits short-term items best.
  \item \textbf{Nothing to fall back on when things go
    wrong.} If an item turns out to be damaged or a seller
    disappears after being paid, there is no escrow, no
    record of the handover and nobody to complain to.
\end{itemize}

CampusXchange tackles each of these. It checks identity
against the college, publishes listings only after review,
stores negotiations as structured offers, holds payment
until a verified handover, and lets an administrator settle
disputes using photographs taken at the meeting.

\section{Project Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Verified access (O1):} Only students with a
    valid \texttt{@thapar.edu} email can join. The database
    enforces this, not just the app. Each account also needs
    a unique roll number and a student ID card that an
    administrator approves before the student can trade.
  \item \textbf{One marketplace for three kinds of deal
    (O2):} A single searchable feed for Sell, Lend/Rent and
    Exchange listings, with category filters and an option
    to sort by distance from the student's hostel.
  \item \textbf{Trust and safety (O3):} No listing appears
    publicly until an administrator has reviewed it.
    Students can also report a listing or another student,
    and an administrator can take a live listing down.
  \item \textbf{Recorded negotiation (O4):} Prices and swaps
    are agreed through structured offers that the system
    stores, delivered to both sides in real time.
  \item \textbf{Escrow and verified handover (O5):} Payment
    stays in escrow until the buyer scans a single-use QR
    code at the meeting. Condition photographs are taken at
    handover and return, and lend periods, deposits and due
    dates are tracked.
  \item \textbf{Dispute handling (O6):} Either side can
    report a problem, which freezes the money until an
    administrator decides the case from the photographs and,
    if needed, applies a fixed penalty.
  \item \textbf{Measurable evaluation (O7):} The system
    records the timestamps needed for the metrics in our
    proposal, especially time to listing clearance (TLC),
    and shows them to administrators.
\end{enumerate}

Our Software Requirements Specification (SRS, version 2.1)
breaks these seven objectives down into 81 functional and 37
non-functional requirements, and every functional
requirement that we built has at least one test case
(Chapter~\ref{ch:results}). The objectives were realistic for
one semester because we used a managed platform for login,
file storage and real-time updates, which left us free to
focus on the marketplace itself.

\chapter{Methodology and Design}

\section{System Architecture}

The system has three tiers: the app that students use, a
backend API that holds the business logic, and a managed
data platform. We kept two paths separate on purpose. Signing
in goes straight from the app to the identity provider,
while every other read and write goes through our backend. A
third, read-only path sends live updates from the database
to the app.

\subsection{High-Level Design}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the system as it is
built and deployed now.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/architecture-deployed.png}
  \caption{High-level architecture as built and deployed.
    Solid boxes are implemented and tested. Dashed boxes are
    simulated in the pilot or still to be set up.}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The main parts work together as follows.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The app.} A single React Native codebase
    serves verified students, guests (who can browse but not
    trade) and administrators, who get an extra
    \emph{Review} tab because of a role flag on their
    account. The same code is built both as an Android app
    and as a website, and the website doubles as the
    administrators' web dashboard.
  \item \textbf{Signing in.} The app asks the identity
    provider for a one-time code and checks it directly. Our
    backend never sees the code or a password. It only
    receives the session token that results, and it checks
    that token on every request.
  \item \textbf{The backend.} A stateless NestJS service with
    eight modules (profile, listings, campus, chat and
    offers, transactions, notifications, reports and admin)
    and 53 endpoints. Each request first passes a security
    layer that adds security headers, limits which websites
    may call the API, applies rate limits and validates the
    input. It then goes through one of three guards. The
    first needs a valid session. The second lets guests in
    but still recognises a signed-in user. The third also
    reads the user's admin role from the database.
  \item \textbf{The data platform.} A managed PostgreSQL
    database with 17 tables, all protected by row-level
    security, plus three storage buckets and a real-time
    channel for messages, offers, deals and notifications.
    ID cards and condition photographs sit in private
    buckets and can only be opened through signed links that
    expire (after ten minutes for ID cards and thirty for
    condition photographs). Listing photos are public, since
    everyone, including guests, needs to see them.
  \item \textbf{Outside services.} An SMTP provider emails
    the sign-in code. Phone notifications are sent through
    Expo's push service; the code for this is written, and
    turning it on for the Android app only needs a Firebase
    project, which we have not set up yet. The payment
    gateway is simulated for the pilot. Escrow holds,
    releases and refunds are all recorded and every rule is
    enforced, but no real money moves.
\end{itemize}

Two details in the figure are worth explaining. The backend
uses a privileged database key that skips row-level security,
so that key lives only in the backend's settings and never in
the app. The app only has the public key, and with row-level
security turned on, that key cannot read or change any table
by itself. Second, guests and signed-in users browse through
the same endpoint. When there is no token, the backend simply
leaves out the meeting location, so a guest can see what is
on sale but not where the handover would happen.

\subsection{Technical Stack}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Framework/Language:} React Native 0.86 with
    Expo SDK 57 and Expo Router for the app, and NestJS 12 on
    Node.js for the backend. Everything is written in
    TypeScript 6 in strict mode, so both tiers share the same
    types.
  \item \textbf{Database:} PostgreSQL, hosted on Supabase,
    which also gives us login, file storage and real-time
    updates. The schema is built by twelve SQL migration
    files, numbered 000 to 011, kept in the repository.
  \item \textbf{Tools:} Git and GitHub for version control,
    GitHub Actions for continuous integration, Vitest for
    unit and end-to-end tests, PostgREST and a PostgreSQL 16
    container to run the end-to-end tests against a real
    database in CI, oxlint for linting, draw.io for the UML
    diagrams and Expo Go for testing on our phones during
    development.
  \item \textbf{Deployment:} The backend runs on Render's
    free web service tier, EAS Build produces the Android
    app, and EAS Hosting serves the website at
    \texttt{campusxchange\allowbreak.expo\allowbreak.app}.
  \item \textbf{Libraries:} the Supabase client libraries,
    \texttt{class-validator} for checking requests,
    \texttt{@nestjs/throttler} and \texttt{helmet} for rate
    limits and security headers, \texttt{expo-camera} for
    scanning QR codes, \texttt{expo-image-picker} for photos,
    \texttt{react-native-reanimated} for the small amount of
    animation in the interface, and
    \texttt{expo-notifications} for push notifications.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System Modelling and UML Diagrams}

Before and during development we modelled the system with
four kinds of UML diagram. Each one answers a different
question. The use case diagram shows what the system does,
the class diagram shows what data it stores, the sequence
diagrams show which messages pass between the parts, and the
activity diagrams show how the workflow branches. We drew
them for the whole system at mid-semester, when payments,
handover and disputes were still to be built, and then built
those parts to match. In practice the diagrams became the
specification for the second half of the project.

\subsubsection{Use Case Diagram}

Figure~\ref{fig:usecase} has four actors (Student, Verified
Student, Administrator and an external Notification Service)
and nineteen use cases.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/usecase.png}
  \caption{Use case diagram: four actors and nineteen use
    cases, grouped by privilege level.}
  \label{fig:usecase}
\end{figure}

The use cases are split by privilege. A student who has
only signed up can upload an ID card, browse and receive
category alerts. Creating listings, negotiating, paying and
raising disputes are only for a Verified Student, meaning
someone whose ID card an administrator has approved. Since
Verified Student is a specialisation of Student, it inherits
everything in the first group. This is how the diagram shows
the central idea of the project: verification decides what
you are allowed to do. The backend enforces the same rule,
because every trading endpoint checks whether the caller is
verified.

\subsubsection{Class and Entity-Relationship Diagram}

Figure~\ref{fig:class} shows the thirteen core tables with
their columns and constraints, generated from the database
schema.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/class-er.png}
  \caption{Class and entity-relationship diagram of the
    thirteen core tables, with primary key, foreign key and
    not-null constraints. The four supporting tables added
    later are described in the text.}
  \label{fig:class}
\end{figure}

Two choices in this model are worth pointing out. First,
\texttt{public.users} is not where identity comes from. Its
primary key is a foreign key to the login provider's own
user table, so our table is a profile attached to an
identity that is managed elsewhere. A database trigger
creates this profile automatically as soon as an account is
made. Second, we drew every foreign key as its own line. The
\texttt{chats} table points to \texttt{users} twice (once for
the buyer who started the chat and once for the owner), and
\texttt{transactions} does the same for buyer and seller. A
single ``participates in'' line would have hidden the fact
that each role has its own rules.

We added four supporting tables in the second half of the
semester. \texttt{campus\_\allowbreak locations} stores the
18 hostels and campus meeting points with their coordinates,
which we use to sort listings by distance.
\texttt{category\_\allowbreak subscriptions} stores category
and want-tag alerts, \texttt{push\_\allowbreak tokens} stores
the phones that receive notifications, and \texttt{reports}
stores reports against listings or students. The
\texttt{listings} table also gained columns that record when
and why an administrator took a listing down.

\section{Implementation Details}

\subsection{Module 1: Verified Access and Identity}

We chose not to build our own login system. Students sign
up with a one-time code sent to their email, so we never
store passwords and don't need to handle hashing or password
resets.

Limiting sign-up to the college took two layers. The app
checks the email address before asking for a code, but on
its own that check means little, because a modified app or a
direct call to the API can skip it. The check that actually
protects the system is a database trigger that rejects any
account whose email does not end in the college domain. The
account is never created and no code is sent. A unique index
also makes sure each roll number can only be used for one
account.

The second part of identity is the student ID card. The
photo is uploaded through the backend into a private bucket,
under a file name the server chooses, and the profile is
marked as waiting for review. An administrator opens it
through a signed link that lasts ten minutes and approves or
rejects it. A rejected student is notified and can upload a
clearer photo. Until they are approved, students can browse
but cannot list, chat or trade, and the server checks this
on every trading request.

\subsection{Module 2: Listings, Search and Moderation}

All listings live in one table, whatever their type. A
\texttt{type} column says whether it is a sale, a lend or an
exchange, and the type decides which other fields are needed:
a price for a sale, a rent amount and unit for a lend, and a
list of wanted items for an exchange. The API checks that the
right fields are present, so a sale without a price is
rejected straight away. A listing can have up to six photos,
and the meeting point has to be one of the 18 hostels and
campus spots from the campus map.

Students can search the feed by keyword, filter it by type
and category, and page through it 20 listings at a time (at
most 50 per request). They can also sort it by distance,
which is the straight-line distance from their hostel to
each listing's meeting point. We don't use GPS for this, so
the app never asks for location permission.

Moderation is built into how the data flows. A new listing is
saved as \texttt{pending\_review}, and an entry is added to
the moderation queue at the same time. The public feed only
shows listings that are \texttt{live}, so there is simply no
way for a student to publish directly. If adding the queue
entry fails, the listing is rolled back as well. When an
owner edits a live listing, it goes back for review.

After mid-semester we added reporting. Any signed-in student
can report a live listing, or a student they have chatted
with, by choosing a reason and adding details. Each student
can have only one open report on the same listing or person.
Administrators see open reports grouped by what was
reported. If they act on a report about a listing, the
listing is taken down for good, and a database trigger
stops it from ever coming back. Any deal on it that has not
reached the handover is cancelled and refunded, all open
reports on it are closed, and the owner, the reporters and
both sides of any cancelled deal are notified.

\subsection{Module 3: Chat and Structured Negotiation}

Each buyer gets one chat thread per listing, which a
uniqueness constraint enforces, and only verified students
can start one. Every message and offer request checks that
the caller is part of the thread. Anyone else gets a
``forbidden'' response.

What makes this more than a messaging app is the offers. An
offer is its own record with a price, a status and the
person who made it. Either side can make an offer, but only
the other side can accept or reject it, and this is checked
on the server, not just hidden in the interface. When one
offer is accepted, all other pending offers in that thread
are rejected automatically, so a thread can never end up
with two agreed prices. For exchanges, the student who wants
to swap names and photographs the item they are offering,
and either side can add a cash top-up and say who pays it.

Messages, offers and deal updates arrive through the
database's real-time channel, with slow polling as a
fallback. The real-time feed uses the student's own session,
so row-level security still decides which rows each phone
receives. The Chats tab marks threads that have something
new and shows a count of unread chats on the tab itself.

\subsection{Module 4: Transactions, Escrow and QR Handover}

When the owner finalises the agreed terms, the system creates
a transaction at exactly the agreed price and confirms the
meeting point. For a lend, it also records the start date,
end date, deposit and daily rent. The listing is then marked
\texttt{reserved}, and the buyer pays into escrow. In the
pilot the gateway is simulated, so the hold is recorded but
no money actually moves.

At the meeting, the person handing over the item shows a QR
code on their phone. The code is random, works only once,
belongs to that one transaction and expires after ten
minutes. If it expires, they can show a new one, and the old
one stops working. The person receiving the item first takes
a photo of it and then scans the code. A valid scan uses up
the code, and for a sale or exchange it completes the
transaction and releases the escrow. Instead of scanning,
either person can report a problem, which marks the
transaction as disputed and freezes the money. Before the
handover, either side can cancel, which refunds the escrow
and puts the listing back in the feed. Once the item has
changed hands, nobody can cancel.

A lend stays open after the handover. The borrower gets a
reminder the day before the due date, overdue items are
flagged, and the return is confirmed with a second QR scan
and a second set of condition photos. If nothing is wrong
with the returned item, the rent is settled and the deposit
is refunded. All due dates are worked out in Indian time
rather than the server's time. Section~\ref{sec:analysis}
explains the bug that taught us to do this.

\subsection{Module 5: Disputes, Notifications and Administration}

Each transaction can have one dispute, with a reason.
Administrators see open disputes with the handover and return
photos side by side. They can ask for more evidence, and they
settle the case by either releasing the money to the seller
or refunding the buyer. They can also apply one of three
fixed penalties (Rs.~50, Rs.~200 or Rs.~500) to whoever was at
fault. Both people are notified each time the dispute
changes.

Notifications are stored for each user with a type, some
details and a read flag. They cover listing decisions,
messages, offers, deal updates, disputes, reports and
category alerts. Once a Firebase project is connected to the
Android build, they can also be sent as phone notifications
through Expo's push service.

Administrators work from the \emph{Review} tab, which only
appears for accounts with the admin role. It has queues for
listings, ID cards, reports and disputes, and a dashboard with
the metrics from our proposal: TLC (median, 90th percentile
and the share decided within 24 hours), listings by type,
completion rate, the share of deals that are lends, the
on-time return rate and dispute rates. Hiding the tab is only
for convenience. The real check is a guard that reads the
user's role from the database on every admin request.

\subsection{Module 6: Security Hardening and Interface}

Before deploying, we deliberately tried to misuse our own API
and then fixed what we found. A photo is now accepted only if
its actual contents are a JPEG, PNG, WebP or HEIC image,
whatever the file name says. Uploads are stopped as soon as
they pass 8 MB, a listing can only use photos its owner
uploaded, and the server picks every file name. Each
signed-in session is rate limited to 120 requests and 30
writes a minute, 20 new listings an hour and 40 photo uploads
every ten minutes. Responses carry standard security headers,
and browser access can be limited to the app's own website.
Continuous integration fails if a package we ship has a known
high or critical vulnerability.

In the second half of the semester we also redesigned the
interface around a sage green, off-white and tomato red
colour scheme with light, slightly see-through cards. We kept
animation to a minimum, and it turns off completely when the
phone's reduce-motion setting is on. Figure~\ref{fig:screens}
shows the main screens, taken from the deployed app on one of
our phones.

\subsection{Module 7: Deployment}

The system now runs without any of our laptops. The backend
is deployed on Render's free tier straight from our GitHub
repository and redeploys whenever we push, with its secret
keys stored in Render's settings. The website, which is also
the admin dashboard, is exported as a static site and hosted
on EAS Hosting. The Android app is built with EAS Build as an
installable file that talks to the deployed backend. A proper
iPhone app would need a paid Apple developer account, so for
the pilot iPhone users can use the website instead.

\chapter{Results and Evaluation}\label{ch:results}

\section{Testing Methodology}

Every functional requirement that we built has at least one
test case; the only one without is FR-AUTH-10, which we did
not build. We named each automated test after the requirement it
checks, for example \texttt{FR-PAY-7 · the buyer or the
seller can cancel before the handoff}, so the test results
can be read directly against the requirements.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unit Testing:} 35 unit tests cover logic that
    can be checked on its own. In the backend these are the
    image content check, the rate-limit rules, campus dates,
    want-tag matching and the guard that only lets verified
    students trade. In the app they cover email validation,
    date handling and distance formatting.
  \item \textbf{Integration Testing:} 344 end-to-end tests
    start the built backend and call it over HTTP as real
    test users, against a real PostgreSQL database created
    from all twelve migrations with row-level security
    switched on. They cover every implemented requirement,
    including access control (calling protected endpoints
    without a token, with a fake token and as the wrong
    user), every state a deal can move through, and the
    security requirements. GitHub Actions runs them on every
    push, along with linting, type checks and a dependency
    audit.
  \item \textbf{Performance Testing:} We timed the main
    endpoints over 200 requests each and loaded the feed with
    ten clients at once for ten seconds
    (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). We still need to load test
    the deployed system with real pilot traffic.
  \item \textbf{User Testing:} 16 manual test cases cover
    things that need a real phone and a real person, such as
    receiving the email code, staying signed in, scanning a
    QR code with the camera, chatting live between two
    phones, checking the screens and receiving phone
    notifications. The team runs these and records the
    tester, date and result in our test-case sheet. Using the
    app on our own phones throughout the project also caught
    several bugs that automated tests could not, which we
    describe in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What we added beyond the plan.} Several features were
not in our original proposal: browsing as a guest with the
meeting location hidden, sorting by distance from your
hostel without needing location permission, swap offers with
photos and a cash top-up, reporting and takedown, and a full
test suite tied to the requirements. Each of these is
recorded in the SRS as a documented change from the
proposal.

\textbf{Where we fell short.} Payments are still simulated.
The escrow rules are complete and tested, but connecting a
real gateway such as Razorpay was not possible in a free,
one-semester project. Photos are not compressed before
upload yet. We also have not run the pilot with real
students, so the proposal's metrics are still unmeasured.

\textbf{Challenges we ran into.} These were the ones we
learned the most from.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Photo uploads failing.} Image uploads kept
    failing with an unclear error. The cause was that the
    mobile framework replaces the standard network code with
    its own version, which does not accept files in the usual
    format. We only worked this out by reading the
    framework's source code, and then switched to its own
    file format.
  \item \textbf{Sign-in emails not arriving.} The platform's
    built-in email service only sends to members of the
    project team, at two emails an hour, and could not send
    the numeric code our sign-in needs. We fixed this by
    using our own SMTP provider and rewriting the email
    templates.
  \item \textbf{Holes we found by attacking our own API.}
    When we tried to misuse the API on purpose, we found that
    it accepted any file that claimed to be an image,
    accepted oversized uploads, and let a listing use another
    user's photos. We fixed all three and added tests for
    them.
  \item \textbf{A time-zone bug.} A test for lend reminders
    only failed after midnight. Dates were being calculated
    in UTC, which is still the previous day between midnight
    and 5:30 am in India, so for those hours due dates and
    reminders were a day early. We now calculate all campus
    dates in Indian time, and the tests pass in both time
    zones.
  \item \textbf{An admin action that skipped the rules.}
    While testing takedowns, we noticed that approving or
    rejecting a listing did not check what state it was in,
    so an administrator could bring a removed listing back.
    Both actions now only work on listings waiting for
    review, and a database trigger keeps taken-down listings
    off the feed.
  \item \textbf{A typing bug on Android.} After the redesign,
    the text boxes on Android kept passing focus from one to
    the next in a loop, so nobody could type. The box changed
    its style when it was tapped, which made Android rebuild
    it and pass focus on. Now the box keeps the same style
    when tapped. This bug never showed up in the web preview,
    only on a real phone.
\end{itemize}

Just like at mid-semester, most of these problems came from
the places where our code meets something else, such as the
framework, the email service, the server's clock or Android's
focus handling, rather than from the marketplace logic
itself.

\chapter{Conclusion}

\section{Summary of Work}

We set out to build a marketplace where everyone is a
verified Thapar student, every listing is checked before it
goes public, and every deal has some protection behind it.
That system is now complete and deployed. A student signs up
with their college email and a one-time code, adds a unique
roll number and a photo of their ID card, and once an
administrator approves them, they can buy, sell, lend and
swap. Listings are reviewed before they go live and can be
reported and taken down later. Prices and swaps are agreed
through structured offers in a real-time chat. The payment
stays in escrow until the buyer scans a one-time QR code at
a campus meeting point. Both sides photograph the item at the
handover and the return, lend periods and deposits are
tracked, and an administrator settles disputes by looking at
those photos.

The system is described by SRS version 2.1 (81 functional and
37 non-functional requirements), modelled with four types of
UML diagram, checked by 379 automated tests and 16 manual
test cases, and deployed for free as an Android app and a
website.

\section{Key Findings}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Finding 1:} A rule that only exists in the
    app is not really a rule. The college email check, the
    admin role, verified-only trading and listing ownership
    only became reliable once we enforced them in the
    database or in a guard on the server.
  \item \textbf{Finding 2:} Modelling caught mistakes before
    we wrote the code. Drawing the lend return as a separate
    branch showed that reusing the sale path would have
    closed lend deals before the item came back. Because we
    built the second half against the diagrams, it held very
    few surprises.
  \item \textbf{Finding 3:} Naming tests after requirements
    made gaps easy to spot. Since every test carries its
    requirement's ID, we could find missing tests, and SRS
    wording that no longer matched the code, just by reading
    one generated table.
  \item \textbf{Finding 4:} Connecting to other systems took
    the most effort. Our hardest bugs came from the
    framework, outside services and the phone platform
    rather than our own code, and some only appeared on a
    real phone or at a certain time of day.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work and Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Run the pilot.} Put the deployed app in front
    of 30 to 40 students and two or three volunteer
    moderators, as planned in the proposal, and report the
    metrics that the dashboard already calculates.
  \item \textbf{Connect a real payment gateway.} Holding,
    releasing and refunding escrow are three functions in
    the backend. Replacing them with a gateway's escrow or
    split-payment feature is the main step needed for real
    money.
  \item \textbf{Over-the-air updates and an iPhone app.}
    Adding Expo's update service would let us send fixes to
    installed phones without a reinstall. Publishing on the
    App Store needs an Apple developer account.
  \item \textbf{Compress photos before uploading} to save
    students' mobile data (NFR-PRF-4).
  \item \textbf{Automatic moderation and roll number checks.}
    An automatic moderation score could help order the review
    queue (without ever rejecting a listing on its own), and
    an official student list, if the college can share one,
    could be used to confirm roll numbers.
  \item \textbf{Load testing and monitoring} of the deployed
    system, to measure response times and uptime under real
    campus traffic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Final Remarks}

The biggest lesson from this project was about where trust
really comes from in software. Every safety promise in our
proposal, such as only real students, only reviewed listings
and money held until the item changes hands, was easy to show
in the interface and much harder to actually guarantee
behind it. Each time, the version that worked was the one
enforced closest to the data.

The second lesson was about proof. The diagrams told us what
to build, and the tests told us whether we had built it.
Naming every test after a requirement turned ``we think it
works'' into a table that anyone can check. The system is not
large, but every promise it makes is written down, enforced
on the server and tested.
